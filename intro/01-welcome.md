# Welcome to the Work Experience Programme

You are about to spend two weeks building real software. Not a tutorial. Not a worksheet. A working application that runs in a browser, talks to APIs, stores data, and does something genuinely useful.

This is what software engineers do every day. By the end of the fortnight you'll have done it too.

---

## What you'll actually be doing

Every team picks one project from a menu of ten ideas — ranging from a mood journal to an AI-powered study tutor to an interactive physics simulation. You'll design it, build it, test it, and present it on the final day.

You'll write real code (JavaScript or Python), use real tools (VS Code, Git, GitHub), work in a real team structure, and ship a real product. The emphasis throughout is on **learning by doing**: you will hit bugs, you will feel confused, and you will figure it out. That process is the point.

---

## Why software engineering?

Software engineering is one of the most versatile skills you can learn. It is not just a career (though it is a great one) — it is a way of thinking that makes you better at almost everything else:

- **Problem-solving**: breaking big problems into small, testable pieces
- **Logic and precision**: computers do exactly what you tell them, not what you mean
- **Communication**: working with other people on complex shared systems
- **Creativity**: software is one of the most creative fields there is — you build things from nothing

The UK tech industry employs over 1.7 million people. Average starting salaries for junior developers are £28,000–£40,000. Senior engineers in London regularly earn over £100,000. But more importantly: the ability to build software gives you leverage in virtually any career, from medicine to music to climate science.

And you don't need a Computer Science A-Level to get started. Many of the best engineers are self-taught or came from other backgrounds. What matters is curiosity and persistence — both of which you already have, or you wouldn't be here.

## What the two weeks look like

| Day | Focus |
|-----|-------|
| Day 1 | Set up tools, meet your team, pick your project, read the brief |
| Day 2 | Plan your sprint: break the project into tasks, assign roles |
| Day 3–4 | Build the core UI and data structures |
| Day 5 | First working version (even if rough) — end-of-week review |
| Day 6–8 | Add features, connect APIs, polish the UI |
| Day 9 | Testing, bug fixes, write your README |
| Day 10 | Final demo, presentations, retrospective |

Each morning starts with a **standup**: three sentences per person, done standing up, taking no more than five minutes. Each afternoon ends with a **check-in**: what's working, what isn't, what you need help with.

## How to pick a project

The ten projects in this programme are arranged roughly by difficulty, from ⭐⭐ (GCSE level) to ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (A-Level stretch). Browse [projects/README.md](../projects/README.md) and ask yourself:

1. **Does it interest you?** The best predictor of finishing a project is caring about what it does. Pick something that excites at least one person on your team.
2. **Is it the right difficulty?** Challenging-but-achievable beats boring-and-easy or overwhelming-and-impossible. If in doubt, go one step harder than feels comfortable.
3. **Do your team's strengths fit?** Some projects are heavier on design, some on logic, some on APIs. Think about who's on your team.
4. **Can you explain what it does in one sentence?** If you can't, the scope might be too fuzzy.

You don't need to know all the technologies before you start. Part of the job is learning them as you go.

## The spirit of the programme

**Mistakes are good.** Every bug you hit teaches you something a textbook can't. Professional engineers spend a large fraction of their time debugging. Getting comfortable with that is part of the job.

**Shipping matters.** A rough working product is worth more than a perfect unfinished one. Aim to have *something running* by the end of Day 5, even if it looks terrible. You can make it good in week two.

**Ask questions early.** If you've been stuck for more than 20 minutes, ask. Not because you failed — because asking is part of the skill. Real engineers ask questions constantly (Stack Overflow, GitHub issues, asking colleagues). The ones who learn fastest are the ones who ask most.

**No one knows everything.** Your mentors will sometimes say "I don't know — let's find out." That's not a cop-out; that's accurate. Every developer spends time looking things up. The difference between a junior and senior developer is mostly knowing *where* to look and *what questions to ask*.

**You belong here.** There is no prerequisite knowledge for this programme beyond curiosity. If you feel out of your depth: good. That's where learning happens.
