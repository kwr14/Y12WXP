0. Why bother?

An LLM — the thing powering modern AI coding agents like Auggie — is, at its heart, a giant pile of numbers that does two things over and over:

  1. Multiplies vectors by matrices.
  2. Compares vectors using dot products.

It learns by following gradients downhill. That's it. By the end of this page you'll understand all four pieces — and you'll have built and dragged them with your own hands.

You don't need any prior maths beyond "I know what an x-y graph is." We'll build everything else.

➡️

1. Vectors: numbers with direction

A vector is just a list of numbers

In 2D, the vector v = (3, 2) means "go 3 right and 2 up." Drag the purple dot below to change v.

v = (3, 2)  ·  |v| = 3.61
💡 Magnitude |v| = √(x² + y²) is the length of the arrow — Pythagoras applied to the vector's components.
In an LLM: every word becomes a vector — but with ~4,000 components instead of 2. Words with similar meaning end up at similar positions in that 4,000-dimensional space.

2. The dot product: how similar are two vectors?

Multiply matching components, then add

v · w = vx·wx + vy·wy

The dot product is one number that tells you how aligned two vectors are. Drag both arrows below and watch the value change:

v · w = ?
try: line them up  ·  make them perpendicular  ·  point them apart
💡 Same direction → big positive number. Perpendicular → exactly 0. Opposite → negative.
In an LLM: "attention" is literally the dot product between a word's query vector and every other word's key vector. Bigger dot product = more attention paid to that word.
Two word-embeddings give a dot product of 0. What does that suggest about the words?

3. Matrices: vectors going through a machine

A matrix transforms space

A 2×2 matrix takes any 2D vector and spits out a new 2D vector. The four numbers control how the grid stretches, squashes, or rotates. Slide them around — the orange square shows where the original unit square ends up.

a: 1.0 b: 0.0
c: 0.0 d: 1.0
M = [[a, b], [c, d]] · v
💡 Try setting M to [[0,-1],[1,0]] — that's a 90° rotation. Or [[2,0],[0,2]] — that scales everything by 2.
In an LLM: every layer is a stack of matrix multiplies. The "weights" you keep hearing about are literally the numbers inside those matrices. Training = adjusting those numbers until the model gets good at predicting the next word.
If you set the matrix to [[1,0],[0,1]] (the identity), what happens to a vector going through it?

4. Derivatives: which way is downhill?

The slope at a point

For a curve y = x², the derivative dy/dx = 2x gives the slope at any point x. Drag the dot along the curve and watch the tangent line tilt:

x = 1.0  ·  y = 1.00  ·  slope = 2.0
💡 Positive slope means the curve is going up as x increases. Negative slope means going down. Zero slope means flat — a peak or a valley.
In an LLM: training measures how wrong the model is (the "loss"). The derivative of the loss with respect to each weight tells you which way to nudge it to get less wrong.
At the bottom of the parabola (x = 0), what is the slope?
⛷️

5. Gradient descent: rolling downhill on purpose

Take a small step opposite the slope

xnew = xold − η · slope

η ("eta") is the learning rate. Press Step to take one step of gradient descent. Try changing the learning rate — too big and you'll overshoot; too small and you'll crawl.

η: 0.30
x = 2.50  ·  loss = 6.25  ·  step 0
💡 The "loss landscape" of a real LLM has billions of dimensions — but the idea is identical: nudge each weight a tiny bit opposite its gradient, repeat trillions of times.

6. Putting it together: how an LLM thinks

Every prompt you type follows this chain

  1. Token → vector. Each word/sub-word becomes an embedding vector (Lesson 1).
  2. Matrix multiply. The model projects that vector into "query", "key", and "value" vectors using learned matrices (Lesson 3).
  3. Dot product. Each token compares its query against every other token's key — that's attention (Lesson 2).
  4. More matrices. The result is mashed through dozens of further matrix multiplies and nonlinearities to produce the next-token guess.
  5. Loss + gradient. During training, the model measures how wrong it was and uses derivatives (Lesson 4) to compute a gradient.
  6. Step downhill. Every weight in every matrix gets nudged opposite its gradient (Lesson 5). Repeat — billions of times — and you have a language model.
That's it. The only "magic" is scale: more parameters, more data, more steps. The maths underneath is what you just dragged around.

Cheat sheet

Vector

List of numbers. Has direction + magnitude |v|=√(Σxᵢ²).

Dot product

v·w = Σvᵢwᵢ. Measures alignment. Zero = perpendicular.

Matrix

A grid of numbers that transforms vectors. Layers of an LLM are matrices.

Derivative

Slope of a function. Tells you which way is "up" — so −slope is "down."

Gradient descent

x ← x − η·∇L. Repeat until loss is small. That's "training."

Attention

Dot products of queries vs keys, scaled and softmaxed, used to weight values.
